Fondazione Montagna Sicura focuses on research and monitoring in high-altitude environments.
In particular, our research activity addresses hydrogeological risk in high mountain areas.

The summit of Mont Blanc holds great symbolic and cultural value,
both on the Italian and the French side.

This led us to collaborate with our French colleagues,
resulting in a scientific monitoring project of the summit ice cap.

The idea came from observations above 4,000 meters,
not only on Mont Blanc, but also on Monte Rosa and other massifs.

In recent decades, changes once limited to lower glaciers
now seem to be affecting even the highest altitudes.

We are starting to collect data today
to understand whether degradation has also begun at these extreme elevations.

Alongside scientific research, this mission included a technological test.
Low temperatures, thin air and strong winds challenge our instruments.

We tested drones and ground-penetrating radar
to understand how and to what extent we can operate in extreme environments.

My name is Martina Lodigiani.
I am a researcher at Fondazione Montagna Sicura, within the PNRR Agile Arvier project.

The survey was conducted over two days.
The first focused on topographic measurements, the second on geophysical surveys.

For the topographic survey, we used a drone
equipped with both photogrammetric and LiDAR sensors.

This allowed us to create a digital model
of the ice cap surface.

For the geophysical survey, we used ground-penetrating radar.
This instrument emits electromagnetic waves into the subsurface.

When these waves reach the bedrock,
they are reflected back to the surface.

The recorded signals provide information
on the thickness of the ice.

Using the collected data, we generated a three-dimensional model
of both the ice surface and the underlying rock.

The rocky summit beneath Mont Blanc
was measured at approximately 4,786 meters.

It is covered by an ice layer about 20 to 22 meters thick.
Above it, the summit was measured at 4,807.3 meters.

The goal was not only to measure the summit height,
but to reconstruct the entire surrounding area.

This will allow future monitoring
of changes and long-term evolution.

A historical series of measurements of Mont Blanc exists.
The most recent surveys are carried out every two years.

These data show irregular variations over time,
but recent years suggest a possible downward trend.

This hypothesis will need to be verified
through future measurements starting from today.

Observing change at such extreme elevations,
once considered stable, highlights the scale of climate change.

It invites reflection on the trends we are experiencing
and on the solutions that must be pursued in the future.
